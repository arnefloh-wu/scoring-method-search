<td>Statistische Methoden</td>
<td>Weighted-sum factor scoring / Nutzwertanalyse (Zangemeister)</td>
<td>Christof Zangemeister: Nutzwertanalyse in der Systemtechnik. München: Wittemann, 1971 (book; DNB record)</td>
<td>[https://d-nb.info/720188229](https://d-nb.info/720188229)</td>
<td>The standard German reference for the scoring model used in most Ländermarktauswahl lectures. Weights: set directly by the decision maker, usually as percentages summing to 100 and often split hierarchically (criterion groups, then sub-criteria). Each country gets a rating on each criterion (e.g. 1-10 points or a normalised indicator). Aggregation: additive, Nutzwert = sum of weight x score. Data: a criteria list, weights and a country-by-criterion score matrix. Strengths: transparent and fits easily in Excel; students can trace every number. Weaknesses: it assumes criteria are independent and fully compensatory (a high market size offsets high political risk); rating scales are arbitrary; results depend on how the data are normalised; it is prone to double counting. Application: Cavusgil's (1997) Market Potential Index, which became the globalEDGE MPI, and most practitioner export-market scoring sheets (WKO, GTAI-style checklists) use this logic. Caveat: the DNB catalogue lists 1971; the book is often cited as 1970, and the 4th edition is 1976.</td>
<td>Lecture anchor and Excel lab base model (students build a 10-country x 8-criterion Nutzwert sheet). Access: book in libraries only; check WU library holdings.</td>
<td>neu; Link geprüft</td>
</tr>

<td>Statistische Methoden</td>
<td>Direct rating and point allocation weight elicitation</td>
<td>Paul A. Bottomley & John R. Doyle, Omega 29(6):553-560, 2001 (journal article)</td>
<td>[https://doi.org/10.1016/S0305-0483%2801%2900044-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0305-0483%2801%2900044-5)</td>
<td>Compares three ways of eliciting weights directly. Direct rating: rate each criterion on a 0-100 scale, then normalise. Point allocation: distribute 100 points across the criteria. Max100 / Min10: anchor the most or least important criterion. Finds that direct rating with the most important criterion anchored at 100 gives more reliable and preferred weights than point allocation. Aggregation is usually a weighted sum. Data: only managers' or students' judgements. For market selection this is the quickest way to get team weights for criteria like market size, growth, distance and risk, but the weights are sensitive to how the criteria are framed and to range insensitivity: weights are stated without reference to how much the countries actually differ. Application: implicitly used in most practitioner country checklists. No dedicated IMS paper found.</td>
<td>In-class warm-up: elicit weights in the room with both methods and compare the resulting country rankings. Access: check WU licence (Elsevier).</td>
<td>neu; in Suchergebnis bestätigt</td>

<tr>
<td>Statistische Methoden</td>
<td>Rank-order centroid (ROC) and rank-sum weights</td>
<td>F. Hutton Barron & Bruce E. Barrett, Management Science 42(11):1515-1523, 1996 (journal article)</td>
<td>[https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.42.11.1515](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.42.11.1515)</td>
<td>How weights are obtained: the decision maker only ranks the criteria. The ranking is converted into surrogate weights. ROC: w_i = (1/n) \* sum over k=i..n of 1/k. Rank-sum: w_i = 2(n+1-i)/(n(n+1)). Rank reciprocal is a third option. Aggregation: weighted sum. Barron & Barrett show by simulation that ROC weights choose the best alternative most of the time. Stillwell, Seaver & Edwards (1981, OBHP, doi:10.1016/0030-5073(81)90015-5) is the earlier comparison of rank-sum and rank-reciprocal weights.
